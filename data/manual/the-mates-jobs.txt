Anybody can make a boat go. Crew earn their keep by making it stop, exactly where the captain wants it. The captain finds the fish. The mate does nearly everything else.

Start with the anchor.

On a small boat the anchor goes out from the bow. A small boat held by its stern alone can be swamped by the current, or by one good wave. A stern anchor only ever goes out together with a bow line, the way the crew ties off for the storm in chapter twelve: a line from the bow to a mangrove, and an anchor out the back, so the boat is stretched tight from end to end.

Let out plenty of line. Five to seven feet of line for every foot of water. That sounds like a lot, and it is. An anchor holds by lying down and digging in, not by hanging straight down like a yo-yo.

To set it, the captain points the boat into the wind or into the current, whichever is stronger, and stops. You lower the anchor. Lower. You never throw it. Then the boat drifts back, and the anchor bites.

Now the dock. The rule here is one of the best sentences ever spoken on the water. Never come at a dock faster than you’re willing to hit it.

Before the boat gets there, your lines are already on the cleats and the fenders are already over the side. And your hands and feet stay inside the boat. Never, ever fend off a dock with your body. The boat weighs a great deal more than you do, and the dock doesn’t move at all.

When you tie up, you’ll use a cleat hitch. Once around the base of the cleat. A figure eight over the two horns. One locking turn. It holds all night, and it comes off in a second.

That’s stopping. Now the other half of the job. The bait and the ice.

Lively bait catches fish. Tired bait feeds crabs.

So the air goes on first. Switch on the aerator, or the livewell pump, before the bait goes in. Bait dies from too little oxygen long before it gets hungry. In the book, a little battery aerator is what keeps the shrimp alive overnight.

Don’t crowd the well. A few lively baits beat a packed crowd of dying ones. If they’re all up at the surface gulping, there are too many in there.

Keep the water cool and fresh, keep the lid shut against the sun, and scoop out anything dead right away.

When it’s time to bait a hook, wet your hands and use the dip net, not your fist. A shrimp that’s been squeezed swims wrong. Fish notice.

And then the ice. Bring more than you think you need. A fish that’s going home for dinner goes straight onto the ice, and ice with a splash of seawater in it chills a fish the fastest. After that, keep the lid shut. Every peek lets the cold out. If the boat has room, drinks ride in one cooler and fish in another, because the drink cooler gets opened every ten minutes, and the fish cooler shouldn’t be.

Do all that, and here’s what happens. The captain turns around to ask for something, and it’s already in your hand. That’s the whole secret of being a good mate. Be one step ahead.

Three questions. Out loud.

One. How much anchor line goes out for every foot of water? [pause] Five to seven feet. The anchor has to lie down to dig in.

Two. Finish the rule. Never come at a dock faster than... [pause] Faster than you’re willing to hit it.

Three. What goes on before the bait goes into the well? [pause] The air. The aerator, or the pump. Air first.
