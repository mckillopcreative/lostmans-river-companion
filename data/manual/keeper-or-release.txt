Here’s a moment that comes on every trip. A guest lands a nice fish, holds it up, grins at you, and says, “Can we keep it?”

And every eye on the boat turns to the mate.

A guide knows the answer cold. Not because a guide loves rules, but because the fish in this river belong to everybody, including whoever fishes here after you’ve gone home. So let’s learn how the answer gets made.

It starts before the trip. For each kind of fish there are usually three things to know. How big it has to be. How many each person may keep. And whether the season is open at all. Now listen carefully, trainee, because this is the one place I’m going to refuse to help you. I am not going to tell you those numbers. They change from year to year, and from one coast of Florida to the other, and a number I say into a microphone today could be wrong by the time you hear it.

So here’s what a guide does instead. Before every trip, you look them up. In Florida the rules come from the Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission. F W C, for short. Then you write today’s rules down. Matt’s Fishing Journal has a page made for exactly that. Put the date on it. If the date is old, check again.

Now the fish is on the deck, and you need its length. This is the part you do with your hands.

One. Wet the board, and wet your hands. Dry hands and a hot deck strip off the slime that keeps a fish healthy.

Two. Mouth closed, nose to the stop. Lay the fish flat on its side, with its closed mouth touching the end of the board.

Three. Pinch the tail. For most of the fish around here, you squeeze the tail fin together and read the longest point. That’s called total length. A few kinds are measured to the fork of the tail instead. The rule for each fish tells you which.

Then you compare your number to the rule. And there’s one idea here that catches people out. It’s called a slot.

A slot limit has two ends: a smallest size, and a biggest size. A fish that’s too short goes back, to grow. Fair enough. But a fish that’s too long goes back too. Why? Because the big ones are the breeders. One big fish makes a great many little ones. So the fish everybody most wants to keep is very often the one the river most needs to have back.

And if the fish is right on the line? Then it’s not a keeper. When in doubt, let it go. The same goes for a fish you won’t actually eat.

So let’s say it’s going back. Do it well. Keep the fish in the water as long as you can, and unhook it there if you’re able. If you lift a big fish, hold it level, with one hand under the belly. Never hang it by the jaw alone. If the hook is deep, don’t dig for it. Cut the line close, and leave it. The fish will do better than you’d think.

And if it’s tired, revive it. Hold it upright in the water, facing into the current, until the tail kicks out of your hand. That’s what Matt does with his big snook in chapter six. He holds it upright, he moves it through the water, and its tail kicks. That’s as good a picture of a guide as there is in the whole book.

Three questions. Out loud.

One. Where does the nose go when you measure a fish? [pause] Against the stop at the end of the board, with the mouth closed.

Two. In a slot limit, which fish go back? [pause] The ones that are too short, and the ones that are too long. The big ones are the breeders.

Three. Where do today’s sizes and seasons come from? [pause] You look them up before the trip. In Florida, that’s F W C. Not from memory, and not from me.
