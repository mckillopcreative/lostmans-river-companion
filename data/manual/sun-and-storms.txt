There’s one more thing on the morning checklist, and it’s the biggest thing out there. The sky.

A mate looks up as often as a mate looks at the water. So here is what you’re looking for.

Start with the pattern. In a Florida summer the mornings are usually calm and clear, and the storms build in the afternoon. That’s why guides leave the dock in the dark. They want to be fishing at sunrise and heading home when the clouds start to stack up.

And you can watch them stack. By late morning, look for tall white clouds piling upward like scoops of ice cream. When the bottoms of those clouds turn flat and dark, the storm is building. If the wind suddenly swings around and blows cool on your neck, it’s close. Say so. The captain has probably noticed. Say so anyway.

Now, lightning. On open water, a boat is the tallest thing for a mile, and a fishing rod is a very good lightning rod. So here is the rule, and there are no exceptions to it. If you can hear thunder, the lightning is close enough to matter.

You can measure how close. When you see a flash, count the seconds until the thunder arrives. Every five seconds is about a mile. If the count is getting shorter, the storm is coming to you.

What happens next is the captain’s call, and a good captain makes it early. The best place to be in a thunderstorm is back at the dock. If there’s no time for that, the rods go down flat, everybody gets low, and nobody holds on to anything metal. In the book, when the storm catches the crew in chapter twelve, they tie the boat off tight in the mangroves and wait it out. Nobody fishes through it. Nobody. And nobody heads back out until half an hour after the last thunder.

The wind matters too, even without a storm. Wind blowing against the tide stands the water up into short, steep waves, the kind that soak everybody and slow the trip home. So the captain plans the way back before going out.

Then there’s the sun. It doesn’t look dangerous. It is. Out on the water it comes at you twice: once from the sky, and again off the surface. So. A hat with a brim. A long-sleeved sun shirt. Sunscreen before you leave, and again every couple of hours. And polarized sunglasses, which do two jobs: they protect your eyes, and they let you see down into the water.

Drink water before you’re thirsty. By the time you’re thirsty, you’re already behind. And watch your crew. A headache, feeling dizzy, feeling sick to the stomach: those are the body’s warning signs for too much heat. If you see it in a guest, or feel it yourself, tell the captain. Then it’s shade and water, right away. And if somebody is confused, or faints, that’s an emergency, and the captain calls for help.

At dawn and dusk the bugs come out, mosquitoes and the tiny biting gnats called no-see-ums. Bring repellent. In the book, Uncle Dan mixes his own, out of lavender.

One last thing, and I hope you never need it. If somebody goes over the side, you have two jobs. First, shout it, loud: “Man overboard!” Second, point at the person in the water, and never take your eyes off them. Not for a second. A head in the water disappears faster than you’d believe. The captain turns the boat. You point.

The guide’s rule for the sky: the fish will still be there tomorrow. Make sure you are.

Three questions. Out loud.

One. You see lightning and count ten seconds before the thunder. About how far away is it? [pause] About two miles. Five seconds for every mile.

Two. A storm is on top of you. What happens to the fishing rods? [pause] They go down flat. A rod in the air is a lightning rod.

Three. Somebody falls over the side. What are your two jobs? [pause] Shout “man overboard,” and point at them without ever looking away.

That’s the boat, and the sky. Next lesson, we meet the fish.
