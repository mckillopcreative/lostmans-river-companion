Starting the engine is the captain’s job. Knowing whether it started right is everybody’s. So here is what a mate watches for.

An outboard engine cools itself with the water it’s sitting in. That one fact explains almost everything.

It’s why the engine gets lowered into the water before it starts, never after. An outboard run dry can ruin its water pump in seconds.

It’s why, as soon as the engine fires, you look over the side for the tell-tale. That’s a thin stream of water squirting out of the engine, and it should show up within a few seconds. It means cooling water is going around. No stream? Say so, right then, and the captain will shut it down. That’s not being a pest. That’s the job.

Before the key turns, the throttle is in neutral, the lanyard is clipped on, and somebody has looked behind the boat. Nobody in the water. No line trailing near the propeller. A loose line and a propeller will always find each other.

Then the engine idles for a few minutes while you stow the gear. A cold engine pushed hard wears out early. And while it warms up, listen. A healthy engine sings one steady note. An alarm, a shake, or a brand-new noise all mean the same thing: stop, and find out why.

The guide’s rule for this one: watch the stream before you watch the fish.

Now you’re moving, so you need the rules of the road.

A channel is a road made of water, and the markers are the lines painted on it. The saying that keeps you in the deep part is three words long. Red, right, returning.

It means this. When you’re coming in from open water, heading home, keep the red markers on your right-hand side. Heading back out to sea, they’ll be on your left.

You can tell the markers apart even when the light is bad. The red ones are triangles, and they carry even numbers. The green ones are squares, with odd numbers. At dusk, when everything looks gray, the shape still tells you the color.

In a channel, keep to the right, the same as a car on a road. If two boats meet head on, both turn to the right.

Then there are slow zones. Idle speed, no wake, means the boat settles all the way down in the water and leaves no wave behind it. In Florida, manatee zones are posted on signs. Read the sign. Do what it says.

And here’s the manners part. The wave behind your boat is called your wake, and it belongs to you. It rocks docks. It rocks kayaks. It rocks the fellow who’s been anchored on his favorite spot since sunrise. So you slow down before you get to him. Not after.

One more, and it will get you out of more trouble than any other rule on the water. When in doubt, slow down. No rule anywhere ever asks you to go faster. Slowing down buys you time to think.

Most of the markers out there went in for the same reason: somebody found that sandbar the hard way first. Be grateful to that person. Don’t be that person.

Three questions. Out loud.

One. The engine has just started. What do you look over the side for? [pause] The tell-tale. A thin stream of water that says the engine is cooling itself.

Two. You’re coming home from open water. Which side do the red markers go on? [pause] The right. Red, right, returning.

Three. It’s getting dark and you can’t make out the color of a marker. But it’s a triangle. What color is it? [pause] Red. Triangles are red. Squares are green.
