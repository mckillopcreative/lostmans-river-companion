A guest swings a fish over the side, turns to you, and says, “What is it?” From now on, you’re the one who answers. So we’re going to learn the six fish that matter most on this river, and we’re going to do it with our ears. Close your eyes if you like.

For each fish I’ll give you the shape, the color, and the one thing that gives it away. Guides call that the tell-tale.

Fish one. The snook.

Picture a long, sleek fish built like a torpedo. Silver sides, an olive-green back, and fins with a touch of yellow. The forehead slopes down to a pointed snout, and the lower jaw sticks out past the upper one, as if the fish is about to argue with you. Now the tell-tale. One thin black stripe, like a line drawn with a marker, running from the gill all the way to the tail. See the stripe, and you know. Nothing else on the river wears it.

Snook are ambush hunters. They wait under mangrove branches, along oyster bars, and at the mouths of rivers. Hook one and it does two things. It jumps, and then it runs straight for the roots to cut you off. And mind the gill cover when you hold one. The edge is as sharp as a blade.

Fish two. The tarpon. The silver king.

Everything about a tarpon is big. A big body, and scales the size of your palm, bright as new coins. The mouth turns upward, like a scoop. And trailing off the back fin there’s one long thread, like a ribbon somebody forgot to cut. That’s your tell-tale, along with the size. You’ll often see tarpon before you ever hook one, because they roll at the surface to gulp air. And when one feels the hook, it leaves the water. All of it.

Fish three. The redfish.

A redfish is copper. Bronze on the back, fading to a white belly, with a blunt nose and a mouth that points down, because it feeds by rooting along the bottom. The tell-tale is at the other end. One black spot, about the size of a thumbprint, at the base of the tail. Sometimes there are two or three. Some people call the fish a spot-tail. In skinny water a redfish will tip up to dig for crabs, and its tail waves in the air. The first keeper Matt ever caught was a redfish.

Fish four. The spotted seatrout.

Slimmer than the others. Silver-gray, with black dots sprinkled over the back, up the back fin, and across the tail, as if somebody shook a pepper shaker over it. Open the mouth, carefully, and there are two fangs at the front of the top jaw. You find trout over seagrass. In the book, grass beds mean trout. And the trout has a soft mouth, so you never yank. A hard pull tears the hook right out.

Fish five. The mangrove snapper.

A stocky, gray-bronze fish with a reddish blush, and often a dark stripe running through the eye, like a bandit’s mask. The name tells you the rest. Snappers snap. They have real teeth, and they’ll use them on your finger. They hold tight to mangrove roots, pilings and deep holes, and they are the best bait thieves on the river.

Fish six. The grouper.

Wide head. Thick body. Blotchy brown, like camouflage. And a mouth so big it looks as if the fish could swallow its own reflection. A grouper sits in a hole, eats whatever passes, and when it’s hooked it has one plan: get back under its rock. Some kinds grow bigger than you are. In the book, something nobody ever sees drags an anchored boat clear across a channel. That’s a grouper story.

Six fish. Stripe, ribbon, spot, pepper, mask, and mouth.

Three questions. Out loud.

One. A long silver fish with one black stripe from gill to tail. What is it? [pause] A snook.

Two. Copper colored, with a black spot at the base of the tail. [pause] A redfish. The spot-tail.

Three. Which fish has the soft mouth, so you never yank? [pause] The spotted seatrout.
