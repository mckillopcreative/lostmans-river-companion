Last time, the six that matter. Now the others that come over the side, usually when you were hoping for something else. A guide can name these too. A guide also knows which ones to keep fingers away from.

We’ll start with the look-alikes.

The black drum. It’s the redfish’s cousin, and it has the same shape: deep body, blunt head, mouth pointing down. But a black drum is gray, not copper. It has no spot on its tail. And under its chin there are whiskers, short soft ones, for feeling out crabs in the mud. Young ones wear wide dark bars down their sides. So: copper with a tail spot, redfish. Gray with chin whiskers, black drum.

The sheepshead. This one also wears bars. Bold black and white stripes, straight up and down, like an old-time prisoner’s suit. Some people call it the convict fish. But the real tell-tale is inside the mouth. A sheepshead has teeth that look human. Front teeth, back teeth, the full set. It uses them to crunch barnacles off pilings, and it is famous for stealing bait without you ever feeling a thing.

So, two fish with bars. Young black drum: whiskers. Sheepshead: people teeth. And the snapper from last time has no bars at all, just the mask through the eye.

The flounder. You can’t mistake this one. It’s flat as a dinner plate, and both of its eyes are on the same side of its head. It lies on the bottom, colored exactly like the sand, and waits. Brown on top, white underneath.

The jack crevalle. A blunt, bulldog forehead, a yellow tail, and a black spot on the gill cover. Jacks hunt in gangs, and they pull harder, pound for pound, than almost anything out there. If the water suddenly boils with fleeing baitfish, there’s a good chance jacks are doing the herding.

The ladyfish. Long, thin, bright silver, and airborne. A ladyfish spends most of the fight out of the water. Fun to catch. Slippery to hold.

The mullet. Blunt nose, silver, about as long as your forearm, and it jumps. For no reason anyone has ever proven. Mullet almost never take a hook. But nearly everything you’re after eats mullet, so when they’re jumping, pay attention.

Now the ones to handle with respect.

The hardhead catfish. Whiskers, a slick gray body, and three stiff spines: one on top, and one on each side. Those spines are sharp, and they carry a sting that will ruin your afternoon. If one comes aboard, don’t grab it. Tell the captain, and reach for the pliers.

The pufferfish. Small, blotchy, and when it’s upset it swells up like a prickly ball. It has a beak that can clip a hook, and the little puffy ones are famously tough to unhook.

The pinfish. Hand-sized, with faint stripes and a row of needles along its back. It’s bait. Hold it from underneath.

And sharks. They belong here, they are faster than you, and they are the captain’s business. If something big and gray takes your bait, say so, and do what you’re told. Remember Uncle Dan’s rule four. “There is such a thing as too much fish.”

Here’s the guide’s rule for every fish in this lesson and the last one. If you can’t name it, you don’t keep it. So learn the names.

Three questions. Out loud.

One. Gray, shaped like a redfish, with whiskers under the chin and no spot on the tail. [pause] A black drum.

Two. Black and white bars, and teeth like a person’s. [pause] A sheepshead.

Three. A catfish comes over the side. What don’t you do? [pause] Grab it. Three sharp spines. Tell the captain, and use the pliers.

Now go and play Fish of the Everglades in Guide School, and see how many of the plates you can name. Next lesson: the neighbors. The ones with feathers, flippers, and far too many teeth.
