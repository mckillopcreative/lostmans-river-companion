This is the listening companion to Escape to Lostman’s River. You know my voice from the book. This time there’s no story. This time I’m talking to you, trainee.

In the book, Matt Creek shows up in the Ten Thousand Islands at fifteen with one suitcase, his father’s fishing guidebook, and a long list of things to learn. By the end of the summer he can rig a line, read a tide, and help a paying guest land a fish. Nobody handed him that. He picked it up the way every mate does: one job at a time, with somebody who knew better standing next to him.

That’s the job you’re training for. Not captain. Mate. The assistant guide. The one who has the bait alive, the lines ready and the net in hand before anybody asks. A good mate is the reason a captain’s day goes right, and every captain you’ll ever meet started out as one.

Here is how the manual works. There are fifteen lessons, and each one is about four minutes long. The first few are about the boat and the sky, because nobody catches anything from a boat that’s stuck on an oyster bar. Then come the fish: what they look like, where they hide, and which ones go back in the water. Then the catching: where to cast, what to tie on, and what to do when something pulls back. We finish with your guests, and with the part of the day nobody puts in the brochure, which is washing the boat.

At the end of each lesson, all but the last, I’ll ask you three questions. Answer them out loud. Yes, out loud, even if you’re in the back seat of the car. Especially if you’re in the back seat of the car. Then I’ll tell you what a guide would say.

Listening is only half of it. Everything in this manual is also in your Experience to play and to print. Guide School has the games. Matt’s Fishing Journal has the pages for the boat. Hear it here, then go and do it there.

Now, two things before we leave the dock.

First. This manual will make you good crew. It will not make you the captain. On a real boat there is a grown-up in charge, and the captain’s word is the last word. If anything I say is different from what your captain says, your captain wins. Every time. Boats and engines are all a little different, and the captain knows this one.

Second. I’m going to teach you how to measure a fish and how to read a rule. I am not going to tell you the rules themselves. Sizes and seasons change from year to year, and a guide looks them up before every trip. I’ll show you where.

Uncle Dan never wrote a manual. He didn’t need one. He has sayings, and you’ve already heard them in the book. They’re the closest thing this river has to a rulebook, so let’s put them up on the wall.

Rule one. “Patience, patience, sunscreen, and patience. Put that on your list.”

Rule two. “Fishing is an endurance sport. Be patient.” You may notice a theme.

Rule three. “A catch for one is a catch for all. Fishing is a team effort.”

Rule four. “There is such a thing as too much fish.” He says that one with a shark on the end of the line. You’ll understand when you get there.

And rule five, the one he pulls out whenever things look a little dicey. “You gotta risk it for the biscuit!”

Notice what’s not on the wall. Nothing about being the strongest, or the best caster, or the one with the fanciest rod. Patience. Teamwork. Knowing when enough is enough. And a little nerve. That’s a fishing guide.

Three questions, trainee. Out loud.

One. In this manual, what job are you training for? [pause] The mate. The assistant guide. Not the captain. Not yet.

Two. If the manual says one thing and your captain says another, who’s right? [pause] Your captain. Every time.

Three. Finish Uncle Dan’s rule. A catch for one is... [pause] A catch for all. Fishing is a team effort.

That’s lesson one. Next: the words. Because a captain can’t yell “grab the thing by the pointy end” in a thunderstorm. Well. He can. It just doesn’t help.
