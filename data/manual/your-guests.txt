So far this manual has been about boats and fish. This lesson is about the hard part. People.

A fishing guide doesn’t get paid to catch fish. A guide gets paid to help somebody else catch one, and to send them home glad they came. Those are different skills. Plenty of great anglers make terrible guides, because they can’t stand to watch somebody else do it badly.

So here’s how a mate looks after the guests.

It starts at the dock. Learn their names, and use them. Ask what they’re hoping for. A first fish? Dinner? A photograph with something enormous? And find out whether they’ve fished before, without making anybody feel foolish for saying no. Everybody on that boat was a beginner once. Including the captain.

The safety talk belongs to the captain. Your part is to show the guests where things are: the life jackets, the drinking water, the sunscreen. Then offer the water and the sunscreen before anyone asks. People on vacation forget they’re cooking. Uncle Dan’s rule one covers it. “Patience, patience, sunscreen, and patience.”

Next: hooks. A hook on the end of a rod is moving fast, and it doesn’t care who it finds. Before any cast, the angler looks behind. Every time. Sunglasses stay on, because they protect eyes as well as cutting the glare. And you, the mate, keep track of where everyone’s rod tip is pointing. If somebody’s about to hook the captain’s hat, you say so. Kindly. Quickly.

Now, teaching. Here’s the order. Tell them. Show them. Then let them do it. And once they’re doing it, the most important rule in guiding comes into play. You never take the rod out of a guest’s hands. Not when they’re doing it wrong. Not when the fish of the day is on. It’s their fish, and their fight. You coach from beside them, one instruction at a time. “Keep the rod up. Good. Now reel.” If you shout five things at once, they’ll do none of them.

Give a beginner the easiest rig in the box, the popping cork. And remember that a small fish is still a fish. To someone who has never caught one, a hand-sized snapper is a trophy. Treat it like one.

Then there are the slow days. You will have them. The tide’s wrong, the wind’s up, and nothing is biting. This is when a guide earns the money. The mood of the crew is the mood of the boat. So you stay cheerful, you keep trying something new, and you point out everything else the river is doing: the osprey with a fish in its feet, the dolphins working the channel, the way the tide is starting to turn. The guidebook Matt carries has a line for days like that. “If you caught fish all the time it wouldn’t be called fishing, it’d be called catching, which is way less fun.”

Never promise fish. Promise effort. You can always deliver effort.

If a guest looks green, or frightened, or too hot, don’t make a show of it. Tell the captain, quietly.

When the weather turns and the captain says the day is over, the guests may grumble. Your job is to back the captain, out loud, in front of them. In the book, Snapper has four words for a hard decision made well. “Tough call. Good call.”

And when the big fish does come: wet hands, hold it level, take the picture quickly, and get it back in the water. A photograph takes a few seconds. After that, the fish has had enough.

At the end of the day, thank them. Remember what they caught, and say it back to them. People remember the mate who remembered their fish.

Three questions. Out loud.

One. A guest is fighting a good fish and doing it all wrong. Do you take the rod? [pause] No. Never. Coach from beside them, one thing at a time.

Two. What does the angler do before every single cast? [pause] Looks behind.

Three. It’s a slow day and nothing’s biting. What can you always promise? [pause] Effort. Never fish.

One lesson left. The part that isn’t in the brochure.
