The water will tell you how deep it is, if you know how to look. A guide reads it the way you read a page.

Start with color. In clear water, dark blue-green means deep. Pale green means thin. Tan or brown means a bar, and it’s probably closer to the top than you’d like. There’s an old rhyme for it. Brown, brown, run aground.

Now the catch. In the Everglades backcountry, color lies. The rivers back there run the color of tea, and tea hides the bottom. So in the backcountry a guide trusts three other things: the chart, the markers, and the depth finder.

Next, watch the surface. A patch of nervous, rippled water sitting in the middle of a calm stretch often means a bar just underneath. A line of foam shows where two currents meet.

Then, watch the birds. If a heron is standing where you’re headed, the water there is probably about shin deep. A heron’s shin. Slow down and go around.

In a river, run the outside of the bend. The current digs the outside of every curve deep, and piles the sand up on the inside.

And remember that the tide changes the map. A flat you floated across at high tide can be dry land four hours later. So before you go into a place, know which way the water is going. You want to know whether the door is opening behind you, or closing.

The guide’s rule: if you’re not sure it’s deep enough, it isn’t.

Now. Let’s talk about the day you get it wrong. Because you will. Every guide in the Everglades has run aground. The good ones did it slowly, and they knew what to do next.

As the water gets thin, the captain trims the engine up. Tilting it lifts the propeller and buys a few inches. And the boat slows down before it needs those inches, not after.

In the thinnest places the engine comes all the way up, and the boat moves by push pole or by trolling motor. That’s how Snapper slides the Mako in among the mangroves without a sound, in chapter nine.

But say it happens. The boat stops, and it wasn’t your idea. Here is the drill.

One. Stop. The captain shuts the engine off. This is the part people get wrong. Gunning the engine to blast off a flat digs a trench through the seagrass that can take years to grow back, and it can wreck the engine too. So nobody guns it.

Two. Lighten and push. The captain tilts the engine up. Everybody shifts their weight toward the deep side. Then push with the pole, back the way you came in. You already know there’s water that way. You were just in it.

Three. Or wait. If the tide is coming in, the cheapest fix in the world is patience. Put the anchor out so you don’t drift even farther onto the flat, and let the water come to you. Have a sandwich. Remember Uncle Dan’s rule two. Be patient.

And here is the rule that saves you from all of it: go in slow enough that you can get out the way you came.

Three questions. Out loud.

One. Finish the rhyme. Brown, brown... [pause] Run aground.

Two. There’s a heron standing right where the captain is headed. What do you say? [pause] Slow down, it’s shallow. Herons don’t stand in deep water.

Three. You’re stuck on a flat. What’s the very first thing that happens? [pause] The engine goes off. Nobody guns it.
