On land, if you say “it’s over there by the thing,” somebody will eventually work out what you mean. On a boat there isn’t time. So everybody on the water uses the same handful of words, and your first job as a mate is to know them cold.

Start at the front. The front of the boat is the bow. The back is the stern. If the captain says “go forward,” walk toward the bow. If the captain says “go aft,” walk toward the stern.

Now the two that trip everyone up. Face the bow. Your left side is port. Your right side is starboard. Here’s the trick: port and left both have four letters. And here’s the part that matters: the words belong to the boat, not to you. Turn around and face the back, and port is still the same side of the boat it always was. That’s the whole reason the words exist.

The body of the boat is the hull. The flat wall across the back, where the engine hangs, is the transom. The top edges of the sides are the gunnels. That word is spelled like “gun wales,” and nobody in the history of boats has ever said it that way. Gunnels. Like tunnels.

Where the captain stands and steers is the helm. On a flats skiff the wheel, the throttle and the key all sit on a console in the middle of the boat.

Draft is how much water the boat needs to float. A flats skiff needs less than a foot, which is why it can slide into places that would park a bigger boat until the next tide.

And last: on a boat, a rope with a job is called a line. It ties off to a cleat, the metal horn bolted to the deck. Call it a rope and somebody’s grandfather will correct you.

That’s the vocabulary. Now the checklist.

Most bad days on the water were decided at the dock, before the engine ever started. So a guide runs the same list every single morning. Even on the thousandth morning. Especially on the thousandth morning.

One. The drain plug. It’s a little plug low in the transom. If it’s not in, the river comes aboard through the back door. Don’t ask whether it’s in. Go and touch it with your own hand.

Two. A life jacket for every person aboard, one that fits, where you can reach it. Wearing it beats looking for it.

Three. The cut-off lanyard. That’s the red cord that clips the driver to the engine’s kill switch. If the driver goes over the side, the engine stops instead of driving off alone.

Four. Fuel, in thirds. A third of the tank to get out. A third to get back. And a third you never plan to use.

Five. Weather and tide. Look at both before you go. In a Florida summer, storms build most afternoons, and the tide decides where you can run and when.

Six. A float plan. That’s a fancy name for telling somebody on land where you’re going and when you’ll be back. In the backcountry your phone may have no signal, so you can’t tell them later. In the book, the crew runs on paper charts for exactly that reason.

Seven. A radio that works, an anchor with plenty of line, and more drinking water than you think you need.

Seven things. But if you only ever remember three, make it these: the plug, the jackets, the lanyard. Touch all three before anybody touches the key.

Three questions. Out loud.

One. You’re facing the bow. Which side is port? [pause] Left. Four letters, both of them.

Two. What’s the flat back wall of the boat called, the one the engine hangs on? [pause] The transom. And the drain plug lives at the bottom of it.

Three. Fuel in thirds. What’s the last third for? [pause] Nothing. That’s the point. It’s the third you never plan to use.
