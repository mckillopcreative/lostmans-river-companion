The fishing is over. The guests are happy, sunburned, and telling the story for the third time. And the mate’s day has about an hour left in it. This is the part that isn’t in the brochure. It’s also the part that decides whether you get invited back.

First, the ramp. A boat ramp is where everybody is watching and everybody is in a hurry. The captain does the driving. You handle the lines and the strap, and you’re the second pair of eyes.

Calm crews get ready somewhere else. Rods stowed, lines sorted, everything unclipped that needs unclipping, in the parking area or at the side dock. Not on the ramp with six trucks waiting.

The trailer backs down slowly. The boat comes on at idle, straight, and the trailer centers it. Nobody guns a boat onto a trailer. Then the winch strap hooks to the bow and cranks up snug, and the safety chain clips on. Both, every time. The engine tilts up before the truck moves, so it can’t drag on the concrete. And everybody stands clear of the trailer and the winch.

Up in the lot: pull the drain plug, strap down the stern, check the trailer lights, and walk once around the whole rig before anybody drives off. The guide’s rule: fast at the ramp comes from slow in the parking lot.

Now you’re home, and you’re tired, and this is the moment that separates crew from passengers. Because salt eats boats. Here’s the list, in order.

One. Fish first. Ice the catch and clean it. Everything else can wait ten minutes. Dinner can’t.

Two. Flush the engine with fresh water, the way that engine’s own manual says to. The water goes on before the engine starts, and stays on until it stops. Never run it dry.

Three. Rinse everything, top to bottom. The deck, the console, the hatches, the outside of the engine. Then the trailer, and most of all its wheels and springs, which went swimming in salt water too.

Four. Rods and reels get a light mist. Never a blast. Hard spray drives the salt inside the reel. Then loosen the drags.

Five. Rinse the lures you used, and let them dry before they go back in the box. One wet hook will rust the whole tray.

Six. Drain the livewell and the cooler, scrub them, and prop them open. Old bait water is tomorrow’s smell.

Seven. Every scrap of trash comes off the boat, and every piece of old fishing line. Loose line tangles birds and turtles.

Eight. Get ready for next time. Fuel. Batteries on charge. The plug where you’ll find it. And tell the captain about anything that’s broken. Whoever washes the boat is the one who knows.

Nine. Write it down. Where you went, what the tide was doing, what rig, what bit. A year from now, that page is worth more than any lure you own.

Then pack for tomorrow. Tonight, not in the morning. The morning you’re hunting for the pliers is the morning the tide won’t wait. The full pack list is in Matt’s Fishing Journal, and it starts with a life jacket that fits.

That’s the job, trainee. All of it. Words, checklists, markers, water, anchors, bait, weather, fish, neighbors, rules, spots, rigs, hooks, people, and a hose.

So what about the big one?

Here’s what the book has to say, and it’s Matt who says it. “You can’t just catch the big one, you have to earn the big one. And you can’t just do it by yourself.”

Every lesson in this manual is the earning part. The plug you checked. The bait you kept alive. The fish you let go because it was an inch over the slot. The guest whose rod you didn’t grab. None of that looks like much on its own. Add it up over a summer, and one morning the tide is right, the rig is right, somebody you trust is standing beside you with the net, and the line goes tight.

When it happens, write it down the same day. There’s a page for it at the back of the journal, with a line for your captain to sign.

No questions this time. Just one job. Finish your missions, earn your Assistant Fishing Guide license, and then get out on the water with somebody who knows more than you do.

And don’t forget rule one. Patience, patience, sunscreen, and patience.

Put that on your list.
