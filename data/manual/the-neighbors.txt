The Everglades is not a fishing hole with some scenery around it. It’s a neighborhood, and you’re the visitor. A guide knows the neighbors by name, and knows the manners that go with each one. Guests love this part. Sometimes more than the fishing.

Let’s go door to door.

Manatees. Big, gray, slow, and gentle, shaped something like a floating potato with a paddle for a tail. You’ll rarely see the whole animal. You’ll see a nose come up to breathe, or a row of smooth round swirls on the surface, like footprints. Those swirls are made by the tail of a manatee swimming just underneath. See them, and the boat slows right down. The manners are simple. Look. Don’t touch, don’t feed, and don’t follow.

Dolphins. They hunt these channels in teams, and they’re better at it than you are. In the book, a playful pair turns up at Lopez River. Enjoy them. But never feed one, and here’s a guide’s secret: when the dolphins move in, the fishing in that spot is usually over for a while. Everything you were hoping to catch has just left.

Alligators. They like the fresher water, back in the rivers. The rule is to give them plenty of room, and the reason is important. A wild alligator is naturally shy of people. An alligator that has been fed loses that shyness, and that’s when it becomes dangerous, to you and to the next person along. So you never feed one. Not a scrap. Not a fish head. And you never clean your catch where one is watching. In the book there’s a gator at Lostman’s River that helps itself to a grouper. It didn’t ask.

Snakes. In the book, a cottonmouth comes to the lantern light at Comer Key. Here’s all the snake knowledge a mate needs. Leave every snake alone. All of them. You don’t have to work out which kind it is. You just have to step back.

Birds. Start with the pelicans. Matt’s uncle has two regulars, Bertha and Oscar. Pelicans are the clowns of the dock, and they’ll steal a bait off your hook in mid-air. Ospreys hover, fold their wings, and hit the water feet first. Herons stand in the shallows like statues. All of them fish for a living, which is why a guide watches them. Birds diving means bait, and bait means fish.

Now and then a bird grabs a bait and gets hooked. Don’t cut the line. A bird that flies off trailing fishing line can tangle in a tree and never get free. Tell the captain. The bird gets reeled in gently, a towel goes over its head to calm it, and the captain deals with the hook.

That brings us to line. Old fishing line and lost nets are the worst things people leave out here. They go on catching for years with nobody at the other end. In the book, an abandoned net traps a baby dolphin. So every scrap of line comes home with you. Yours, and anybody else’s you find.

Then there are the little neighbors. Mosquitoes, and the gnats you can’t see until they’ve bitten you. Snapper has a line about that. “Mosquitos will suck us dry, but they aren’t as dangerous as smugglers.” He has a point. Bring the repellent anyway.

And last, the neighborhood itself. Mangroves. Those tangled red roots standing in the water are the nursery for most of the fish in this manual. Baby snook, baby snapper, baby tarpon all grow up hiding in there. No roots, no fish. So a guide never breaks them, never ties a boat where it will tear them up, and never leaves anything behind in them.

The guide’s rule: leave every place a little cleaner than you found it, and every animal exactly as wild.

Three questions. Out loud.

One. You see a row of round, smooth swirls on the surface, like footprints. What made them? [pause] A manatee, swimming just underneath. Slow the boat right down.

Two. There’s an alligator watching you clean fish. Do you toss it a scrap? [pause] Never. A fed alligator loses its fear of people.

Three. A pelican gets hooked. Do you cut the line? [pause] No. Tell the captain. Reel it in gently, and cover its head with a towel.
