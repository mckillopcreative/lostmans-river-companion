Open a guide’s tackle box and you’ll find a hundred things. On most days, about eight of them get used. Picking the right one isn’t magic. It comes down to a few questions a guide asks before the first cast. How clear is the water? How deep? How windy? Is the tide moving?

Here are the seven rules of rigging. They’ll get you most of the way.

One. Can’t see your lure a foot down? Then the fish can’t either. Give them noise, flash, or smell.

Two. Clear and calm? Go quiet, light and natural. Anything splashy sends fish running.

Three. Skinny water, or grassy? Go weedless, or you’ll catch salad on every cast.

Four. Deep, or the tide is ripping? Add weight until you can feel the bottom.

Five. Wind in your face? Throw something small and heavy that cuts through it.

Six. Dawn, dusk, or a gray sky? Look up. Low light is topwater time.

Seven. Water not moving? Slow down, or go and find water that is.

Now the rigs themselves. Eight of them.

The popping cork. A cupped float, up to two feet of leader underneath, a hook, and a live shrimp. You pop the rod tip twice, and wait. The pop sounds like a fish feeding, and the shrimp hangs right above the grass. It’s made for stained water and a breezy chop, and it’s the easiest rig there is for a beginner. When the cork goes under, reel. It’s how Matt catches his first snook.

The jig and paddle tail. A weighted hook with a soft plastic tail that thumps as it swims. Cast it, let it sink, and hop it back. It’s what you throw to cover water when you don’t know where the fish are, and it cuts through wind.

The free-lined live bait. Leader, a small circle hook, a live shrimp. No cork. No weight. Nothing else. You cast up-current and let the tide carry it under the branches, the way real food arrives. It’s the quietest rig in the box, for clear water and for fish that have seen everything.

The knocker rig. An egg-shaped sinker slides down the line until it knocks against the hook. Drop it straight to the bottom of a deep hole, and wait for the thump. Snapper, sheepshead, grouper.

The weedless soft plastic. A slim plastic bait with the hook point tucked back inside it. It lands softly and slides through grass. It’s for water under two feet deep, and for tailing redfish. Cast past the fish. Never on its head.

The gold spoon. One piece of curved, shiny metal with a weed guard. Cast it a mile, and reel just fast enough to feel it wobble. Muddy water, wind, and hunting redfish across a big flat.

The topwater plug. It floats, and you twitch it so it zig-zags across the surface. Guides call that walking the dog. Dawn, dusk and cloudy days. There isn’t much in fishing more exciting than the explosion when a fish hits it.

And the big one. Heavy leader, a big circle hook, a big live bait. That’s tarpon tackle, and nothing else.

Every one of those rigs is held together by a knot, and a knot is the one thing I can’t teach you through your ears. You need your fingers and a piece of line. So here’s your homework. In your Experience there’s a page called Knots, with five of them drawn step by step. Learn the first one, the improved clinch. It was Matt’s first knot too. And wet it before you pull it tight, every time. A dry knot scorches the line, and then it breaks on the fish of your life.

Three questions. Out loud.

One. The water is muddy and the wind is up. Quiet rig, or noisy rig? [pause] Noisy. A popping cork or a gold spoon. If they can’t see it, make them hear it or feel it.

Two. Thick grass, and less than two feet of water. What kind of rig? [pause] Weedless. Or you’ll be catching salad.

Three. What do you do to a knot before you pull it tight? [pause] Wet it.
