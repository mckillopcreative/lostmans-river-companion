Here’s the secret that separates a guide from a person with a boat. Fish are not spread around the water evenly, like raisins in a cake. The old saying is that ninety percent of the fish are in ten percent of the water. So most of the river, most of the time, is empty.

The trick is knowing which ten percent. And it always has the same two things. Something to eat, and somewhere to hide. Find a spot with both, and you’ve found fish. Let me give you six of them.

One. Mangrove points. Anywhere the shoreline pokes out into the current. The tide sweeps bait around the tip, and fish sit in the quiet water just behind it, waiting for dinner to be delivered. Snook, redfish and snapper. Cast up-current, and let your bait swing past the point the way real food would.

Two. Creek mouths. When the tide falls, it drains the flats behind a creek, and every shrimp and minnow back there has to leave through one small door. The fish know which door. Be there for the last half of the falling tide.

Three. Oyster bars. Crabs and shrimp hide down in the shell, so redfish, sheepshead and black drum come to eat. Fish the edges, where the bar drops off into deeper water. And mind the oysters. They cut fishing line, and they cut feet.

Four. Grass flats and potholes. Seatrout cruise over the grass. A pothole is a pale, sandy patch in the middle of the dark grass, and redfish and snook lie along its edge to ambush whatever crosses the open sand. Drift across with the wind, and cast ahead of the boat.

Five. Deep holes and passes. Deep water is cooler in summer and warmer in a cold snap, and the current carries food through all day. Snapper, grouper, black drum. This is where tarpon roll.

Six. Docks and pilings. Shade, barnacles, and a place to get out of the current. Fish the up-current side and the shady side first.

Those are the places. Now the signs. A guide’s eyes never stop moving, and here is what they’re looking for.

Birds diving. Something underneath is pushing bait to the top. Go and look. Slowly.

Bait showering. Minnows spraying out of the water like a handful of thrown gravel. They’re being chased. Cast to the far edge of the spray.

A wake with no boat. A big fish moving just under the surface pushes a ripple in front of it. Cast ahead of that wake. Never right at it.

Tails. A redfish with its nose down in the grass waves its tail in the air. It’s eating, and it can’t see you.

Jumping mullet. They’re not what you’re after. But everything that eats them is nearby.

And a seam. A line of foam, or a change in the color of the water, where two currents meet. Bait collects along it, and fish patrol it like a fence.

Last thing, and it matters more than any of the rest. The tide. Moving water feeds fish. When the water stops, at the very top or the very bottom of the tide, the fish mostly stop eating. That’s called slack water, and it’s for eating your own lunch. Uncle Dan’s rule of thumb is the hour on either side of high tide, when there’s plenty of water over the oyster bars.

So: food, cover, and moving water. That’s the ten percent.

Three questions. Out loud.

One. What two things does a good fishing spot always have? [pause] Something to eat, and somewhere to hide.

Two. You see a wake moving along with no boat making it. Where do you cast? [pause] Ahead of it. Never at it.

Three. The tide has stopped dead. What’s that called, and what’s it good for? [pause] Slack water. It’s good for lunch.
