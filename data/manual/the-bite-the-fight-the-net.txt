Most fish that get away are lost in the first two seconds. Not during the long fight. Right at the start, when something thumps the line and your whole body wants to yank. Sometimes yanking is right. Often it’s exactly wrong. It depends on what’s tied to the end of the line.

So, five ways to set a hook.

One. The circle hook. A circle hook curves around on itself, and it’s built to catch in the corner of the fish’s mouth. If you jerk it, you pull it straight out. So you don’t jerk. You just reel. Reel until the line comes tight and the rod bends, and the hook does its work by itself. It’s the hardest thing in fishing to believe. It works anyway.

Two. A jig, a spoon, or a plain hook. Reel down until you feel the fish, then make one firm sweep of the rod, up and to the side. Firm. Not wild. You’re setting a hook, not starting a lawn mower.

Three. The popping cork. When the cork goes under, there’s a lot of loose line between you and the fish. That slack has to go first. So reel fast until you feel weight. Then lift.

Four. The topwater plug. This is the hard one. The splash happens before the fish actually has the lure. If you swing at the splash, you pull it away. So you keep working the plug as if nothing happened, until the rod really pulls. Then lift. Everybody strikes too early once. Most people do it a hundred times.

Five. The tarpon. Come tight, and hang on. And when it jumps, you bow. That means you push the rod tip toward the fish, the way you’d bow to a king. It gives the fish a little slack. If you don’t, it lands on a tight line and breaks you off.

Now you’re hooked up. Here’s the fight.

Keep the rod bent. A bent rod is a spring that soaks up every lunge. A straight rod points right at the fish, and then there’s nothing between you and a broken line.

Let the drag do its job. The drag is the brake inside the reel that lets line slip out under pressure. When the fish runs, let it run. When it stops, you reel. Never crank against a running fish.

To gain line, lift the rod slowly, then reel as you lower it. Lift, then reel down. Lift, then reel down.

If the fish heads for the roots, the pilings, or the oyster shell, lean the rod the other way, low to the water, and steer it out. You are not stronger than a big snook. But you can be more stubborn.

And then the landing, which is the mate’s moment. The net goes into the water first, and holds still. The angler leads the fish to the net. Head first. Always head first. A frightened fish swims forward, so it swims right in. Never stab at a fish with the net, never chase it from behind, and never, ever grab the line with your hand.

When the fish is in the net, look at the angler’s face. That’s what guiding is for. Uncle Dan said it best. “A catch for one is a catch for all.”

Three questions. Out loud.

One. You feel a bite on a circle hook. Do you jerk? [pause] No. Just reel until it comes tight.

Two. A tarpon jumps. What do you do with the rod? [pause] Bow. Push the tip toward the fish, and give it slack.

Three. Which end of the fish goes into the net first? [pause] The head. Always the head.
